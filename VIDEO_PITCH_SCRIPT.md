## How the deck order maps onto the guideline structure

The guidelines recommend problem → solution → demo → technical → potential. Your
deck runs solution → technical → problem → scalability → demo → business. That
is a different sequence, and it can still hit every guideline band — but only if
the narration does two specific jobs the slides don't do on their own.

**Job one: the problem is carried by slide 1, not by a problem slide.** Slide 1
is a title card with no body text competing for attention, which makes it the
best problem slide you have. You hold on it for 38 seconds and let the voice do
the work. Judges are told the problem before they see anything else — which is
what the guidelines actually care about, regardless of which slide is on screen.

**Job two: slide 5 becomes the impact beat, not a second problem statement.** By
the time it appears, judges already know the farmer's problem. So slide 5 answers
a different question — *how many people is this?* The two statistics do that in
one line, and the section stays short.

The result: problem-and-impact lands across slides 1 and 5 (~48s combined),
solution on 2 (42s), technical on 3–4 (36s), demo on 7–11 (69s), potential and
closing on 6, 12, 13, 14 (35s). Every guideline band is satisfied without moving
a single slide.

**The one real cost of this order:** slides 3 and 4 explain the architecture
before the judges have seen the product work. Mitigate it by keeping the
technical section concrete and short — say what the pieces *do*, not what they
are — and by making the demo pay those two slides off explicitly. Slide 11's
trace is the callback; that is why the demo ends there.

## Delivery notes

1. **Rehearse to 3:50, not 4:00.** Live pitches run long, never short. If you
   finish a practice run at exactly 4:00, you will be cut off in the room.
2. **The four sentences that must survive if you are running over:** the hook
   (slide 1), the monitoring agent (slide 2), "the money is plain Python" (slide
   4), and the closing (slide 14). Everything else is compressible.
3. **What to cut first if you are 15 seconds long,** in order: slide 9 (the
   disease demo is the most self-explanatory of the five), then slide 6's second
   clause, then the farm-profile recital on slide 7.
4. **Slide 1 is 38 seconds on a static title card.** That feels much longer than
   it is when you are nervous. Rehearse this slide alone until it is comfortable —
   the temptation to advance early will cost you the entire problem statement.
5. **One speaker for the pitch, whoever is steadiest.** Handoffs cost 3–5 seconds
   each and you have 33 seconds of total buffer. Split the *Q&A*, not the pitch.
6. **Assign Q&A owners now.** The guidelines say the most relevant member fields
   each question. Fill this in before the final:
   - Problem, impact, users, adoption → _______
   - Architecture, agents, how the AI works → _______
   - Data, grounding, limitations → _______
   - Business model, roadmap, scale → _______

## Deck fixes to make before the final

These are places where the deck states something the codebase does not support.
Technical implementation is 20% of the score, and this is exactly where a judge
probes.

1. **Slide 4, "Whisper" chip.** Speech-to-text actually runs on OpenAI
   `gpt-4o-transcribe`, with the farmer's UI language passed as a hint;
   `whisper-1` is only the fallback, because it rejects Bengali outright with an
   HTTP 400. Relabel the chip **"OpenAI Speech-to-Text"** — or keep it and be
   ready to explain, since "we measured four configurations and Bangla accuracy
   went from 81% to 97%" is a strong answer, not a weak one.
2. **Slide 4, "DAM API" chip.** The Department of Agricultural Marketing
   publishes no API. Prices are ingested in advance by a script that submits
   DAM's own CSRF-protected commodity report form. Relabel to **"DAM price
   data."** Anyone who knows Bangladeshi agri-data will catch "API."
3. **Slide 5, "Voice-first" chip.** Voice *input* is live and verified. Spoken
   *replies* are not wired in — text-to-speech needs a multi-gigabyte torch
   install that is not in requirements. **"Voice-enabled"** is the defensible
   word.
4. **Slide 11's caption reads "knowledge base had nothing — 0 web results used
   instead."** That depicts your fallback failing, on the one slide whose job is
   to prove grounding works. It is also currently accurate for the wrong reason:
   `TAVILY_API_KEY` is unset, so web search returns nothing. Either set the key
   and regenerate the slide with a real fallback result, or relabel the branch to
   describe the path — "escalates to web search with a cited source" — rather
   than a zero-result run.
5. **Slide 12.** The BDApps CaaS payment gate is a real sandbox charge, but the
   expert desk returns a stand-in reply today. Say "the payment rail is real; the
   expert panel is what we onboard during the pilot." Never imply a live roster.
6. **Slides 7–11 are stylized mockups, not screenshots.** The shipped app is
   chat-first with a live dashboard — crop cards, season timeline, financial
   breakdown, trace log, alerts feed, weather widget — and there is no separate
   "Crop Advisor" page with that sidebar. Either regenerate them from real
   screenshots, or keep the live app open in another window so you can answer
   "is that the real product?" by showing it in five seconds.
7. **The monitoring agent has no slide of its own** — and it is the single
   strongest differentiator in the deck. Every other capability here is something
   a competitor could claim. If you can add one slide, add it between 10 and 11:
   the alerts feed showing a fertilizer date pushed four days because of a real
   forecast. If you cannot, the slide-2 sentence is carrying it alone, so deliver
   that sentence like it matters.

## Accuracy guardrails

Non-negotiable. Fabricated numbers are the first thing a judge checks, and this
project's own documentation makes honesty about what is and isn't built an
explicit value.

- **Never claim a user base, adoption figure, accuracy percentage, or yield
  gain.** There is no deployed user base and no field pilot. The correct answer
  is "we have not measured that in the field yet — that is exactly what the pilot
  is for," and it is a stronger answer than an invented number.
- **The season plan's cost and profit figures come from static Bangladesh
  reference constants,** not a live price feed. Call them "transparent reference
  estimates." The DAM data is a separate feature and does not price the season
  plan.
- **Do not present the marketplace, the sell/store/wait price verdict, or
  supplier comparison as working.** They are placeholders.
- **If you demo a monitor check live, keep the simulated/test label visible.**
  The adjustment logic downstream is real; the trigger is synthetic.
- **The ~40% and ~11% figures** are approximate national statistics (BBS Labour
  Force Survey / World Bank). Keep the tilde, and name the source if asked.

# 3-Minute Judge Q&A

Roughly six to eight questions fit in three minutes. Keep every answer under 30
seconds: lead with the direct answer, add one supporting fact, stop. Do not
narrate the codebase.

### "Why did you choose this problem?"

Agriculture is 40% of the workforce and 11% of GDP, and the information a farmer
needs already exists in public national documents — it is just unusable. That
makes this an access-and-interpretation problem, not a data-collection problem,
which is exactly what an LLM is good at. We deliberately chose it over something
like yield prediction, which would need field sensors we don't have.

### "How is this different from an agri chatbot, or from Krishi Call Centre?"

Three ways. A chatbot answers the question you asked; we produce a dated, costed
plan for one specific plot. A chatbot forgets; our plan is a durable record that
a second agent keeps monitoring after you close the app. And a chatbot asserts;
every claim we make carries a visible trace of the tool call and source document
behind it. The call centre is human, excellent, and open limited hours — we are
available at 2am and remember your last conversation.

### "How does the AI actually work?"

A LangGraph orchestrator classifies each message and routes it to only the nodes
it needs. For a planning request it fans out — the live Open-Meteo forecast and a
pgvector similarity search over our indexed agronomy corpus run in parallel —
then joins into crop ranking, season planning, and financials. The LLM does
language understanding, retrieval-grounded reasoning, and tool selection. It does
not do arithmetic; that is a pure Python function, so the numbers are
reproducible.

### "What stops it from hallucinating?"

Three layers. Retrieval-augmented generation over four real Bangladesh
references, so the model reasons over retrieved text rather than memory. A rule
that the general Q&A agent must make a real tool call before stating any
checkable fact. And every financial figure is computed in code, not generated.
When the knowledge base has nothing we fall back to a real web search with a
cited URL, and when a tool fails we say so rather than filling the gap.

### "What is in your knowledge base?"

1,243 embedded chunks from four public Bangladesh references: BARC's *Hand Book
of Agricultural Technology*, the *Fertilizer Recommendation Guide 2018*, the
*Soil Fertility Atlas 2020*, and the *Yearbook of Agricultural Statistics 2020*.
Stored as vectors in PostgreSQL with pgvector.

### "Is the prototype actually functional, or is this a mockup?"

Functional and deployed. Chat, crop ranking, season planning, financials, disease
detection, the trace log, the Bangla toggle, and the monitoring agent all run end
to end. *(Have the live app open on a second window.)* The deck visuals are
designed illustrations of real flows — the working system is right here, and I
can show you in ten seconds.

### "Who are your users? Will a farmer really use this?"

Smallholders with one to five acres and a smartphone — plus the DAE extension
officers who advise them, who are arguably the faster adoption path since one
officer covers hundreds of farms. For farmers without smartphones, the same
conversation agent is already reachable over SMS and USSD through BDApps, which
is why SMS/IVR is on the roadmap rather than an app-only future. And Bangla voice
input means literacy is not a barrier to asking a question.

### "What is your business model?"

Free advisory, paid expert access. The premium tier is a call with a certified
agriculturist, charged through BDApps direct debit against mobile balance — no
card, no bank account, which matters enormously for this user. Two more tiers
follow: per-seat licensing for NGOs and cooperatives managing many farms, and
verified placement for input suppliers once the marketplace is real. We are
deliberately not monetizing farmer data.

### "How does this scale?"

Technically: the agents are stateless per turn with all state in Postgres, so the
API scales horizontally, and the monitor is a scheduled sweep, so cost grows with
active farms rather than with traffic. The real cost driver is LLM tokens per
conversation, which prompt caching and routing simple intents to smaller models
cut substantially. Geographically: the only Bangladesh-specific pieces are the
indexed corpus and a district coordinate table.

### "What are its limitations?"

Four, honestly. The season plan is priced from static reference constants, not a
live market feed. The marketplace and the sell/store/wait price verdict are
placeholders. Spoken replies aren't wired in — voice input works, text-to-speech
doesn't. And we have zero field validation: no farmer has planted a season on our
advice yet, so we can't claim an accuracy or yield number, and we won't.

### "What happens if the AI gives advice that ruins someone's harvest?"

That risk is why the trace and the sources are *in* the product rather than
hidden behind it — a farmer or an extension officer can see exactly which
document and which forecast produced a recommendation, and overrule it. For the
pilot, advice goes out alongside a DAE officer rather than instead of one, and
the premium tier exists precisely so a human expert is one tap away on a
high-stakes call.

### "What did you build during the hackathon versus before it?"

*(Answer honestly and specifically — judges do ask this, and the git history is
public. Fill in your actual split before the final.)*

### "What happens next if you win?"

A field pilot across one upazila's farmers and their DAE officers, measuring the
one thing we can't claim today: input savings and plan adherence against a
control group. In parallel, SMS/IVR so it works without a smartphone, and real
market price integration so the profit projection stops being an estimate. The
system is deployed and running now — the roadmap is expansion, not rescue.
